# Bento fuelling rules: evidence review and recommended changes (November 2026)

Bento's structure is sound, but four of its numbers are too aggressive for recreational athletes. It gives 1–2.5 h sessions up to 90 g/h when the evidence supports 30–60 g/h. Its sweat model ignores pace and power, so it overestimates fluid needs for slow runners. Its fluid ceiling is too high for running. It treats indoor sessions without a fan like outdoor riding. The highest-impact fixes are an intensity × duration carbohydrate matrix, pace- and airflow-aware sweat estimates, a running fluid ceiling of 750–800 ml/h, and cumulative (not sliding-hour) delivery checks.

## TL;DR
- **Carbs:** replace the duration-only cap with an intensity × duration matrix. Use 0 g/h under 45 min and for zone 2 under 90 min. Use 30–60 g/h for 1–2.5 h. Allow 60–90 g/h only beyond about 2.5 h or for races. Make 90–120 g/h opt-in for gut-trained athletes, because no trial shows a performance gain above 90 g/h.
- **Fluid and sodium:** cap planned running intake at 750 ml/h (500–600 ml/h for slower runners or races over 4 h). Make sweat rate depend on pace or power and on airflow (×1.3 indoors with no fan). Treat sodium as "season to taste" unless the session lasts more than 4 h or planned fluid exceeds about 70% of sweat loss.
- **Delivery and caffeine:** schedule against cumulative targets, not a sliding-hour cap. The current rule turns a 65 g/h target into 40 g/h. Allow caffeine from 30% of the session, but never in the final 30–40 min, and keep the 3 mg/kg default.

## 1. Executive summary

**What Bento gets right**
- Duration bands at the extremes match ACSM/AND/DC 2016: none under 45 min, and up to 90 g/h beyond 2.5–3 h. [drugfreesport](http://drugfreesport.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Position-stand-on-Nutrition-Athletic-Performance-ACSM-2016-1.pdf) [thefeed](https://thefeed.com/insider/new-guidelines-for-nutrition-and-athletic-performance)
- A gut-training ceiling, multiple transportable carbohydrates above 60 g/h, and a 3 mg/kg caffeine default all sit within the ISSN range of 3–6 mg/kg. [springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12970-020-00383-4)
- The default sweat sodium of 900 mg/L (≈39 mmol/L) is close to the normative mean of 36.1 mmol/L in Barnes et al. 2019 (n = 1303). [tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2019.1633159) [mysportscience](https://www.mysportscience.com/post/how-much-do-you-sweat)
- The fluid model replaces less than sweat loss (70%), which is the right direction for hyponatraemia safety.

**What Bento should change (ranked by impact on recreational athletes)**

| Rank | Change | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Carb matrix by intensity × duration. 1–2.5 h endurance → 30–60 g/h (target 45). Over 90 g/h is opt-in only. | The 90-min endurance ride currently gets 75 g/h, about 1.7× the evidence-based target, which raises GI risk for little or no gain. |
| 2 | Add pace (run) or power/RPE (ride) to the sweat estimate. Cap running fluid at 750 ml/h, and at 600 ml/h for runs over 4 h. | The current model gives a 4:45 marathoner the same sweat rate as a 3:00 marathoner (1.25 L/h). Slow runners who overdrink are the main hyponatraemia group. [nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15829535/) |
| 3 | Fix delivery: schedule from cumulative intake, check ±15 g against cumulative target, and allow one dose of overshoot in any 60 min. | The sliding-hour rule systematically under-delivers (a 65 g/h target delivers 40 g/h with 40 g gels). |
| 4 | Add an indoor airflow question (none / fan / strong fan). With no fan, sweat ×1.3; with a fan, ×1.1. | Fan-less indoor sessions cause materially larger sweat losses even at lower work rates. [nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35025720) |
| 5 | Sodium: required only when the session is ≥4 h or planned fluid is ≥70% of sweat. Otherwise use 0 to palatability levels. Lower the ceiling to 1000 mg/h. | Sodium has no performance effect unless it improves fluid balance. Replacing close to 100% of losses can push plasma sodium above the normal range. [trainerroad](https://www.trainerroad.com/forum/uploads/short-url/sachm9POb7MWBZosKDWa87ISAL4.pdf) |

## 3. Question groups

### A. Carbohydrate

**A1. 1–2.5 h sessions**
- **Best estimate:** 30–60 g/h (target 45) for endurance-intensity sessions of 75–150 min in recreational athletes. Use 60–80 g/h only for race intensity ≥2 h.
- **Evidence:**
  - Thomas, Erdman & Burke 2016, joint ACSM/AND/DC position (*MSSE* 48(3):543–568, doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000000852; also *J Acad Nutr Diet* 116:501–528). Their bands: under 45 min "not needed"; 45–75 min "small amounts, including mouth rinse"; 1–2.5 h 30–60 g/h; over 2.5–3 h "up to 90 g/h". [researchgate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/295847724_Position_of_the_Academy_of_Nutrition_and_Dietetics_Dietitians_of_Canada_and_the_American_College_of_Sports_Medicine_Nutrition_and_Athletic_Performance) [drugfreesport](http://drugfreesport.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Position-stand-on-Nutrition-Athletic-Performance-ACSM-2016-1.pdf) [thefeed](https://thefeed.com/insider/new-guidelines-for-nutrition-and-athletic-performance) (Consensus, H.)
  - Jeukendrup 2014, *Sports Med* 44(Suppl 1):S25–33, doi:10.1007/s40279-014-0148-z. A duration-based ladder. The figure caption states the recommendations "are for well trained athletes. Aspiring athletes may need to adjust these recommendations downwards." [researchgate](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/The-new-carbohydrate-intake-guidelines-Carbohydrate-intake-recommendations-during_fig1_262050749) This directly supports lower values for Bento's users. (Narrative review.)
  - Stellingwerff & Cox 2014, *Appl Physiol Nutr Metab* 39:998–1011, doi:10.1139/apnm-2014-0027. Systematic review of 61 studies (n = 679): 82% showed a significant benefit. The benefit grows with exercise duration. In short exercise, "the type and (or) amount of CHO… appear completely irrelevant". (Systematic review, H; mostly trained males.) [cdnsciencepub](https://cdnsciencepub.com/doi/abs/10.1139/apnm-2014-0027)
  - Smith et al. 2013, *MSSE* 45(2):336–341, doi:10.1249/MSS.0b013e31827205d1. 51 recreationally trained cyclists and triathletes, 2 h constant load plus a 20 km TT (≈160 min total), 0–120 g/h of 1:1:1 glucose:fructose:maltodextrin. Estimated gains were 1.0 / 2.0 / 3.0 / 4.0 / 4.7% at 9 / 19 / 31 / 48 / 78 g/h, with diminishing returns above 78 g/h. [semanticscholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Curvilinear-dose-response-relationship-of-%280-120-Smith-Pascoe/6e8e165b2d7e31ae8c390ab051c3c15f658ef701) [umt](https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/curvilinear-dose-response-relationship-of-carbohydrate-0-120-ghsu/) [nutrition](https://jn.nutrition.org/article/S0022-3166%2826%2900091-X/fulltext) (Single multicentre RCT, M.)
  - Podlogar et al. 2022, *Eur J Appl Physiol* 122:2393–2401, doi:10.1007/s00421-022-05019-w. 11 highly trained male cyclists (VO2peak 62.6 mL/kg/min), 3 h at 95% of gas-exchange threshold (sample details from a secondary summary; the primary page could not be opened). At 120 vs 90 g/h, exogenous oxidation in the last hour was 1.51 vs 1.29 g/min, but endogenous oxidation did not differ (2.15 vs 2.20 g/min). Oxidation efficiency was lower at 120 g/h (≈76% vs ≈86%).
  - Wilson 2025, *Sports Med* 56:295–313, doi:10.1007/s40279-025-02372-6. Narrative review: among the few trials comparing ≥100 g/h with 60–90 g/h, "there is not clear evidence that it reduces reliance on endogenous carbohydrate stores or improves performance". [fisiologiadelejercicio](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/A-Narrative-Review-of-the-High-Carbohydrate.pdf) In one trial, TT power was ≈5% higher at 90 than at 112.5 g/h. [springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-025-02372-6) [nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12982284/)
  - Podlogar & Wallis 2022, *Sports Med* 52(Suppl 1):5–23, doi:10.1007/s40279-022-01757-1: "it cannot be recommended to alter the currently recommended maximal carbohydrate ingestion dose… (i.e., up to 90 g·h⁻¹)." [springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-022-01757-1) [gssiweb](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/dietary-carbohydrate-and-the-endurance-athlete-contemporary-perspectives)
- **Disagreement:** The 90–120 g/h literature is oxidation data from trained men over ≥3 h. It has no performance benefit shown and is not about 1–2.5 h sessions. [nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12982284/) [springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-022-01757-1) [nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35951130/) Smith 2013's optimum of 78 g/h comes from ≈160 min sessions, which is the top of Bento's 75–150 min bucket, not 90 min. [umt](https://umimpact.umt.edu/en/publications/curvilinear-dose-response-relationship-of-carbohydrate-0-120-ghsu/)
- **Should the cap depend on intensity?** Yes. Carbohydrate dependence and the benefit rise with intensity. Low-intensity sessions under 2 h rarely deplete glycogen. (Mechanistic plus consensus, M.)
- **Verdict:** Change. Implement this matrix (g/h, range / target). The 1–2.5 h cells are anchored to ACSM 2016; the intensity split is synthesis (PC).

| Duration | z2 / easy | endurance / long run / fondo | tempo / intervals | race |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| <45 min | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 45–75 min | 0 | 0 | 0–30 / 15 | 0–30 / 20 |
| 75–150 min | 0–30 / 0 if ≤90 min, else 20 | 30–60 / 45 | 45–60 / 50 | 60–80 / 70 |
| >150 min | 30–60 / 45 | 60–90 / 70 | 60–90 / 70 | 60–90 / 75 (90 if gut "trained"; 90–120 opt-in) |

Then apply the sport factor, then the gut ceiling (60 / 75 / 90).

**A2. Zone 2 / easy under 90–120 min**
- **Best estimate:** 0 g/h by default. Optional 20–30 g/h only for a stated goal: gut training, a second session the same day, back-to-back days, or starting fasted.
- **Evidence:** ACSM 2016 says intake is "not needed" under 45 min and that small amounts or mouth rinse help in 45–75 min sessions, with the latter evidence drawn from higher-intensity work. [nih](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4008807/) [thefeed](https://thefeed.com/insider/new-guidelines-for-nutrition-and-athletic-performance) Stellingwerff & Cox 2014 found the benefit scales with duration. [cdnsciencepub](https://cdnsciencepub.com/doi/abs/10.1139/apnm-2014-0027) Gut training (Costa et al. 2017, *APNM* 42:547–557, doi:10.1139/apnm-2016-0453): 25 recreationally competitive runners (10 F, 15 M) took 90 g/h for 2 weeks. GI symptoms fell 60% (gel) and 63% (food), and 1-h distance improved 5.2% and 4.3% vs −2.1% for placebo. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1139/apnm-2016-0453) [nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10610183/) This justifies a gut-training toggle, not a default.
- **Grade:** M for "no performance need"; L–M for the gut-training benefit.
- **Verdict:** Change. Remove the 40 g gel from the 50-min zone 2 ride. Add a "goal" toggle (gut training / fasted start / double day) that schedules 20–30 g/h.

**A3. Tempo, gran fondo, ultra**
- **Tempo/intervals:** 60–75 min → 0–30 g/h (mouth rinse or a sports drink is enough). 75–150 min → 45–60 g/h. Grade M (Jeukendrup 2014; ACSM 2016). The endurance band is too high for short interval sessions.
- **Gran fondo:** The endurance band is defensible, but use the >150 min row (60–90, target 70). Grade M.
- **Ultra:** needs its own band; see A4.

**A4. Ultras (6–30 h)**
- **Best estimate:** 30–70 g/h (target 50), plus protein 5–10 g/h, from mixed real food and sports products. Hold the target constant and expect achieved intake to drift down.
- **Evidence:** Tiller et al. 2019, ISSN position stand, *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 16:50, doi:10.1186/s12970-019-0312-9: "150–400 Kcal·h⁻¹ (carbohydrate, 30–50 g·h⁻¹; protein, 5–10 g·h⁻¹) from a variety of calorie-dense foods". It also notes sodium may be needed at "> 575 mg·L⁻¹". [nih](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6839090/) Per Wilson 2025, newer guidance encourages "individual experimentation with carbohydrate intakes of up to 90 g h⁻¹ in ultraendurance sports". [fisiologiadelejercicio](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/A-Narrative-Review-of-the-High-Carbohydrate.pdf)
- **Disagreement:** Position-stand values (30–50 g/h) are below current elite practice (60–90+ g/h). No verified data were found on whether intake should be planned to fall in later hours (unverified; do not model).
- **Fat:** no performance role during the event beyond tolerability and energy density (Tiller 2019, PC).
- **Verdict:** Change. Add an ultra band of 30–70 g/h. Default target 50 (60 if gut "trained"). Add protein 5–10 g/h as a packing-list suggestion. Schedule by aid-station segment (see E).

**A5. Absolute vs body-mass scaling**
- **Best estimate:** keep absolute g/h. Exogenous oxidation is limited by intestinal transport (Podlogar 2022: a plateau at 1.3–1.5 g/min whatever the dose), not by muscle mass. [nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12982284/)
- **Grade:** M (mechanism) and PC. No verified trial scales intake to body mass or power.
- **Verdict:** Keep absolute. Optional: lower the intensity band one step for athletes under 55 kg on long sessions. This is synthesis (PC) and should not be tested as evidence-based.

### B. Delivery

**B1. Dose size, spacing, sliding hour**
- **Evidence:** Lab protocols that achieved 90 g/h used 30 g every 20 min (Costa 2017). [doi](https://doi.org/10.1139/apnm-2016-0453) Hearris et al. 2022 (*J Appl Physiol* 132(6):1394–1406, doi:10.1152/japplphysiol.00091.2022) found comparable exogenous oxidation at 120 g/h whether taken as drink, gel, chew or a combination: "nine trained males ingested 120 g CHO·h−1 from fluid (DRINK), semisolid gel (GEL), solid jelly chew (CHEW), or a coingestion approach (MIX)", with peak exogenous oxidation of 1.56–1.66 g/min. No study was found showing that a 60-min peak above target is harmful when the session average is on target (no evidence = L).
- **Verdict (PC, implementable):**
  - `interval_min = clamp(dose_g / target_gph × 60, min, 40)`, where min = 15 (bike) or 20 (run).
  - Schedule the next item when `cumulative_target(t) − delivered(t) ≥ 0.5 × next_dose_g`.
  - Constraint: any rolling 60 min ≤ `target_gph + max_single_dose` (max_single_dose = 40 g bike, 30 g run).
  - Allow a "half gel" item for runners.
  - Test: 40 g gels with a 65 g/h target over 3 h must deliver 180–210 g (60–70 g/h), not 120 g.

**B2. How precise does delivery need to be?**
- Smith 2013's curve is steep below about 40 g/h (≈1% performance per 10 g/h) and nearly flat from 60 to 90 g/h. [semanticscholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Curvilinear-dose-response-relationship-of-%280-120-Smith-Pascoe/6e8e165b2d7e31ae8c390ab051c3c15f658ef701) ±10 g/h near the target is immaterial. Falling more than 20 g/h below target in sessions over 2 h matters.
- **Verdict:** Acceptance tolerance is the larger of ±10 g/h or ±15% of target, measured over the full session. Grade M (single dose–response RCT).

**B3. Running vs cycling**
- **Cadence:** every 20–30 min running, every 15–30 min cycling. Doses ≤30 g per item running, ≤40 g cycling (PC; Costa 2017 used 30 g every 20 min in runners, and GI symptoms were still common at 90 g/h before training). [doi](https://doi.org/10.1139/apnm-2016-0453) [outsideonline](https://www.outsideonline.com/health/nutrition/new-science-training-your-gut/)
- **Water with gels:** take 150–250 ml with each gel. Tiller 2019 recommends ultra-runners drink "150–250 mL" at regular intervals (exact interval not verified). [unlv](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/context/kns_fac_articles/article/1316/viewcontent/TillerN_etal_InternationalSocietyofSportsNutritionPositionStand_20191126.pdf) Diluting a 25–40 g gel in about 250–500 ml approximates a 6–10% solution (practitioner synthesis, PC; no RCT found that quantifies GI benefit by water volume).
- **Verdict:** Change. When a gel is scheduled, also schedule ≥150 ml of fluid within ±5 min, counted against the fluid plan rather than added on top.

### C. Fluid and sodium

**C1. 900 ml/h for a 3-h road marathon at 18 °C**
- **Evidence:**
  - Sawka et al. 2007, ACSM position stand (*MSSE* 39(2):377–390, doi:10.1249/mss.0b013e31802ca597): "drink ad libitum from 0.4 to 0.8 L·h⁻¹, with the higher rates for faster, heavier individuals competing in warm environments and the lower rates for the slower, lighter persons competing in cooler environments." Also: "it is inappropriate to use a single fluid replacement rate for all runners." [medscape](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/717055_6)
  - Almond et al. 2005, *NEJM* 352:1550–6, doi:10.1056/NEJMoa043901: 488 non-elite Boston Marathon runners. 13% had hyponatraemia (≤135 mmol/L) and 0.6% critical (≤120). Risk factors were weight gain, longer racing time and BMI extremes. Female sex was not an independent risk factor. [nejm](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa043901) [nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15829535/)
  - Hew-Butler et al. 2015, third EAH consensus (*Clin J Sport Med* 25(4):303–320, doi:10.1097/JSM.0000000000000221): drink to thirst to prevent EAH. [sciencedaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/06/150629100926.htm) [eurekalert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/809627)
- **Assessment:** For a genuine 3:00 runner (≈14 km/h, ≈1.1–1.3 L/h sweat by heat-balance synthesis), 900 ml/h is physiologically below loss but above the ACSM range and hard to tolerate while running. For a 4:45 runner, Bento predicts the same 1.25 L/h, but the heat-balance estimate is ≈0.75 L/h (PC). 900 ml/h would then exceed sweat loss, which is the Almond weight-gain pattern.
- **Verdict:** Change. Running ceiling 750 ml/h. Ceiling 600 ml/h if projected duration >4 h. Hard constraint: planned fluid ≤ sweat estimate − 200 ml/h. Show a hyponatraemia warning for any run over 4 h. Cycling ceiling 1000 ml/h (PC; sweat rates on long hot rides often exceed 1 L/h, and drinking on the bike is easier). Grade M.

**C2. Planned vs thirst**
- **Evidence:** In McCubbin et al. 2020, the SDA position statement (*IJSNEM* 30(1):83–98, doi:10.1123/ijsnem.2019-0300), a cited meta-analysis found planned intake "did not further improve performance beyond that of ad libitum drinking, even with BM losses up to 3.1%". The gap "may increase with exercise duration, particularly under heat stress". Ad libitum drinking "does not necessarily guarantee" protection against overdrinking. [humankinetics](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/ijsnem/30/1/article-p83.xml)
- **Verdict:**
  - 1–3 h: present fluid as a guide ("about X ml/h, drink to thirst").
  - Over 3 h, or in heat: plan it, with the ceilings above.
  - Grade M.

**C3. Indoor / treadmill without airflow**
- **Evidence:** In a fan-cooling RCT (Fernandez, Wimer et al., "Fan Cooling Improves Submaximal Exercise Capacity in an Indoor Thermoneutral Environment", *Res Q Exerc Sport* 94(1), doi:10.1080/02701367.2021.1946467; 16 recreationally active adults, 8 M/8 F, about 40 min at 70% HR reserve, thermoneutral room, fan ≈4 m/s), "body mass loss was attenuated (p < .05) with fan cooling (−0.4 ± 0.2 kg) compared to the non-fan trial (−0.6 ± 0.3 kg)". That is ≈50% more loss without a fan, despite a lower workload (103 ± 36 W without vs 118 ± 44 W with the fan). A survey of indoor cyclists (Peeters, Coussens, Spears & Jeffries 2024, *Front Sports Act Living* 6:1433368, doi:10.3389/fspor.2024.1433368) found that 78% of participants attempted to control temperature, with 96% of these using at least one fan. No study was found that quantifies treadmill airflow effects directly (unverified).
- **Verdict:** Change. Add airflow input: none ×1.3, one fan ×1.1, strong fan ×1.0, applied to the sweat estimate (PC, conservative relative to the ≈1.5× observed). Default to "one fan".

**C4. Sweat-rate prediction**
- **Gonzalez et al. 2009** (*J Appl Physiol* 107:379–388, doi:10.1152/japplphysiol.00089.2009): heat-balance equations. The piecewise form is `sweat (g·m⁻²·h⁻¹) = 147 + 1.527·E_req − 0.87·E_max`. Developed largely from heat-acclimatised, military-style datasets. [physiology](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/pdf/10.1152/japplphysiol.00089.2009) [physiology](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/japplphysiol.01056.2011) The invited editorial (Jay & Webb 2009) puts the error at about ±100 g·m⁻²·h⁻¹. [physiology](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/pdf/10.1152/japplphysiol.00556.2009)
- **Barnes et al. 2019** (*J Sports Sci* 37(20):2356–2366, doi:10.1080/02640414.2019.1633159): normative data, not a prediction model. 1303 athletes (1103 M, 200 F). Mean sweat sodium ≈36 mmol/L (≈830 mg/L). [tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2019.1633159) [mysportscience](https://www.mysportscience.com/post/how-much-do-you-sweat)
- **Jay et al. 2024a**, outdoor (Jay, Périard, Clark et al., *J Appl Physiol* 2024;136(6):1478–1487, doi:10.1152/japplphysiol.00831.2023): 102 athletes (64 M, 38 F), 182 running and 158 cycling trials in Australia, about 60 min each in warm weather. Inputs are estimated metabolic rate and environment. Several components are proprietary; a public calculator exists (sweatratecalculator.com).
- **Jay et al. 2024b**, indoor treadmill/ergometer (Jay, Périard, Hunt et al., *J Appl Physiol* 2024;137(4):1014–1020, doi:10.1152/japplphysiol.00829.2023, PubMed 39205641): validated at 20–40 °C, 14–60% RH, ≈40–85% peak aerobic power. Mean absolute error 0.01 L/h (cycle) and 0.02 L/h (treadmill). 95% limits +0.25/−0.22 L/h and +0.33/−0.29 L/h.
- **Comparison:** Bento's model has no metabolic-rate term, which is the dominant driver in every validated model. Its temperature coefficient (+3%/°C) and RH term could not be verified against any source (unverified, PC).
- **Verdict:** Change. Short term: replace the intensity multiplier with a metabolic term (PC formula):
  - Run: `M_W = 4.2 × mass_kg × speed_m_s` (≈1 kcal/kg/km).
  - Ride: `M_W = power_W / 0.22`. If power is unknown, map z2 / endurance / tempo / race to 1.5 / 2.0 / 2.6 / 3.0 W/kg.
  - `SR_L_h = clamp((M_W − W_ext − D) / 675, 0.15, 3.5)`, where D is dry heat loss. Use D = 250 W outdoors at 15–20 °C, scaled −10 W per °C above 20 °C, and 100 W indoors with no fan.
  - Calibrate D against the Jay 2024 calculator for the scenario set.
  - Medium term: implement Gonzalez's E_req/E_max approach, or license the Jay equations.
  - Always prefer the user's measured rate.

**C5. Sodium**
- **Evidence:**
  - McCubbin 2025, *Performance Nutrition* 1:11, doi:10.1186/s44410-025-00011-9: plasma sodium "will generally rise during exercise unless > ~70% of fluid losses are replaced". A specific sodium need arises mainly when "exercise duration >4 h and sweat rate <1800 ml·h⁻¹". Otherwise, "season to taste". Palatability is best when added sodium is ≤100 mg/100 ml. Fluid replacement over 100% is not recommended. Trying to replace 100% of sodium losses can push plasma sodium above the reference range (worked example: 148 mmol/L). [trainerroad](https://www.trainerroad.com/forum/uploads/short-url/sachm9POb7MWBZosKDWa87ISAL4.pdf)
  - SDA 2020: losses range from ∼50 to 3,500 mg/h. Sweat sodium 11–87 mmol/L. Too little evidence to quantify replacement for performance or cramp. [humankinetics](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/ijsnem/30/1/article-p83.xml)
  - Sawka 2007 relays IOM guidance of 20–30 mEq/L (≈460–690 mg/L) in sports drinks. [medscape](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/717055_6) [medscape](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/717055_6)
- **Assessment:** Bento's 50% rule combined with 70% fluid gives ≈640 mg per L of fluid at 900 mg/L sweat sodium. This sits inside the IOM range, so the ratio is fine. The problems are where the rule applies and the ceiling.
- **Verdict:** Change. `required = duration ≥ 240 min OR fluid/SR ≥ 0.7`. If required: `Na_mg_h = min(0.5 × SR × sweatNa, 1000)`. If not required: 0, with "optional: up to 500 mg/h to taste". Remove the 1500 mg/h ceiling (PC). Grade M.

### D. Caffeine

- **Best estimate:** 3 mg/kg total (range 2–6) as a single pre-session dose or split doses. Late doses work.
- **Evidence:**
  - Guest et al. 2021, ISSN position stand (*JISSN* 18:1, doi:10.1186/s12970-020-00383-4): ergogenic at 3–6 mg/kg. The minimum may be "as low as 2 mg/kg". 9 mg/kg brings frequent side-effects. 60 min pre-exercise is the "most commonly used timing". Gels and chews work. [scholaris](https://utoronto.scholaris.ca/items/c8e98e87-7f36-4552-bee1-4a04479490e2) [nih](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7777221/)
  - Talanian & Spriet 2016 (*Appl Physiol Nutr Metab* 41(8):850–855, doi:10.1139/apnm-2016-0053): 15 well-trained male and female cyclists/triathletes who did not normally use caffeine. Caffeine was taken at 80 min of a 120-min ride, then a ≈30-min TT. TT times were 26:36 at 200 mg (≈3 mg/kg), 27:36 at 100 mg (≈1.5 mg/kg) and 28:41 on placebo. [gssiweb](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/caffeine-and-exercise-performance-an-update) [mysportscience](https://www.mysportscience.com/post/2016/08/02/timing-of-caffeine-intake-in-long-races) [sponser](https://sponser.ch/en/blogs/sport-ernaehrung-gesundheit/caffeine-in-endurance-sports)
- **Assessment:** The 40% rule is roughly right for long sessions but wrong for short ones: a 60-min race would get caffeine at 24 min, too late to peak. No evidence supports splitting as better than a single dose; both work (L–M).
- **Verdict:** Change.
  - ≤90 min: whole dose pre-session (≈45–60 min before) or at the start.
  - >90 min: up to 50% at the start, the rest from 30% of duration onward, with no caffeine in the last 30 min.
  - Keep 3 mg/kg default, 6 mg/kg max, and the 400 mg absolute cap. The 400 mg figure is regulatory consensus and was not verified in this review.
  - Add a warning for sessions ending within 6 h of bedtime (PC).

## 5. Caveats
- Most mechanistic evidence for high carbohydrate intake (90–120 g/h) comes from small samples of trained or highly trained men (11 in Podlogar 2022, 9 in Hearris 2022). Smith 2013 and Costa 2017 are the main recreational and mixed-sex datasets. [nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10610183/)
- The sweat-model coefficients in C4, the airflow multipliers, the gut ceilings of 75/90, the 1000 mg/h sodium cap and all scenario best values are this report's synthesis (PC). Calibrate them against measured data before shipping as "evidence-based".
- These details could not be verified: the exact Tiller 2019 drinking interval; Bento's +3%/°C and RH coefficients; and any evidence for altitude or trail carbohydrate bonuses. The Podlogar 2022 sample details come from a secondary summary because the primary page could not be opened.
- Full citation details (author, year, journal, DOI) are given inline in sections 2–3. Two items are identified only by title or PubMed/PMC ID and need verification before formal citation: the 2026 *Journal of Nutrition* review "From Metabolism to Medals", and the review "Fuelled or Fooled? … Ultra-High Carbohydrate Intake" (PMC13615066). [nutrition](https://jn.nutrition.org/article/S0022-3166%2826%2900091-X/fulltext) [researchgate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/406367410_Fuelled_or_Fooled_Examining_the_Evidence_and_Mechanisms_Behind_Ultra-High_Carbohydrate_Intake_in_Endurance_Athletes)
